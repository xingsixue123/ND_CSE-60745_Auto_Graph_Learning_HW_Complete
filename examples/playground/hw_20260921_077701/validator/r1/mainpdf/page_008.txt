Ex. 2.6 (5 pts) — Zombie processes
A zombie is a process that has already run exit() but has not yet been reaped. Its address space,
open files and kernel stack are gone; all that is left is its process-table entry, holding the exit status,
the pid and the CPU time it used.
The kernel keeps that entry because the status belongs to the parent, and the child cannot report
it once it no longer exists. The pid must stay allocated for the same reason: reusing it while the
parent can still name it in waitpid would let the parent wait on an unrelated process.
The cost is one process-table slot and one pid — no memory, no CPU time, no descriptors. A
zombie also cannot be killed, since it is already dead: SIGKILL is discarded, as no execution context
is left to receive it. In ps it appears in state Z, with its command shown as <defunct>.
It disappears when the parent calls wait or waitpid, or when the parent itself exits and init inherits
and reaps it. A long-lived parent that forks children and never waits leaks table entries until fork
fails with EAGAIN, which is why such a parent should handle or explicitly ignore SIGCHLD.
An orphan is the complement: a child still alive when its parent dies. init adopts it and reaps it
when it eventually exits.
Ex. 2.7 — Three papers from the “Scheduling” group
Paper 2-3 — Waldspurger and Weihl, Lottery Scheduling (OSDI 1994)
Summary.
Lottery scheduling represents resource rights as tickets and settles each quantum by
drawing one at random: a client holding t of T tickets wins with probability p = t/T, so throughput
tracks the allocation, no non-zero holding can starve, and a reallocation takes effect at the very next
decision. The abstractions built on the draw matter more than the draw itself. Ticket transfers
hand a blocked client’s rights to whoever is computing on its behalf, dissolving priority inversion
much as priority inheritance does. Currencies denominate tickets locally and convert through an
exchange rate into a conserved base currency, so inflation inside one trust boundary cannot leak
out. Compensation tickets multiply a client’s funding by 1/f when it used only a fraction f of
its quantum, so a thread that yields early for I/O is not quietly punished. A Mach 3.0 prototype
(25 MHz MIPS DECStation, 100 ms quantum) reports a 2:1 Dhrystone allocation yielding 25378
against 12619 iterations/s (2.01:1), an 8:3:1 database allocation reproduced in response time as
well as throughput (17.19, 43.19 and 132.20 s), and currencies insulating one user’s inflation from
another (aggregate ratio 1.01:1 before, 1.00:1 after).
Critical judgment.
The evaluation’s soft spot is one the paper half-concedes. Individual 60-
second runs scatter badly, a 10:1 allocation being observed at 13.42:1, and the authors’ reply is
that large ratios “converge toward their allocated values over longer time intervals”, offering a 20:1
allocation that averaged 19.08:1 over three minutes. That answer costs them their framing: the
introduction argues interactive systems need control “at a time scale of milliseconds to seconds”,
and the related-work section dismisses fair-share schedulers precisely for delivering fairness only “on
a time scale of minutes”. Randomisation makes the error fall only as 1/√n, their own coefficient
