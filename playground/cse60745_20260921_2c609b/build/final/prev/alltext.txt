Problem 1. Benchmarks, baselines and evaluation metrics for graph
embedding on homogeneous graphs
A homogeneous graph G = (V, E) carries a single node type and a single edge type: the type-
mapping functions ϕ : V →A and ψ : E →R satisfy |A| = |R| = 1. Optionally each node carries
an attribute vector xv ∈Rd and a label yv. Graph embedding learns a map f : V →Rk with
k ≪|V | such that proximity in Rk reflects proximity in G; the embeddings are then evaluated on
• node classification — predict yv for held-out nodes, and
• link prediction — score unobserved pairs (u, v) so that true missing edges rank above
non-edges.
The heterogeneous counterpart (multiple node/edge types, metapath-based methods) is the subject
of Problem 2 and is deliberately not covered here.
Statistics in Table 1 were obtained by loading each graph and counting; the OGB figures in Table 2
are from the official OGB documentation.
Where the literature reports conflicting values, the
discrepancy is footnoted rather than silently resolved.
1. Benchmark datasets
1.1 Citation networks (the default benchmarks)
Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed (Sen et al., Collective Classification in Network Data, AI Magazine
29(3), 2008; split protocol from Yang, Cohen & Salakhutdinov, Revisiting Semi-Supervised Learning
with Graph Embeddings, ICML 2016) are the three Planetoid citation graphs and remain the default
testbed for semi-supervised node classification. Nodes are scientific publications, an undirected edge
joins two papers when one cites the other, and the label is the paper’s topic.
Cora collects machine-learning papers and uses binary bag-of-words features.1 CiteSeer is the
sparsest of the three and has a number of isolated nodes, which is why absolute accuracies are
lowest on it. PubMed collects diabetes-related biomedical abstracts and, unlike the other two,
uses real-valued TF–IDF weights rather than binary indicators. All three are also standard link
prediction benchmarks: the GAE/VGAE protocol hides 5% of edges for validation and 10% for
test, samples an equal number of non-edges, and reports AUC and AP.

1.3 Web-page and heterophilous benchmarks (the counterexamples)
The benchmarks above are strongly homophilous, and a GNN that simply averages over neighbour-
hoods does well on them almost by construction. The suite assembled by Pei et al. (Geom-GCN,
ICLR 2020) and popularised by Zhu et al. (Beyond Homophily in Graph Neural Networks, NeurIPS
2020) exists to break that assumption.
WebKB (Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin) are university web pages linked by hyperlinks, with binary
bag-of-words features and 5 page classes (student, project, course, staff, faculty); they are very small
and very heterophilous, so variance across splits is large and results must be averaged over the 10
standard splits. Actor is the actor-only induced subgraph of a film–director–actor–writer network,
where an edge means two actors are co-mentioned on the same Wikipedia page. Chameleon and
Squirrel are Wikipedia page–page networks on those topics, with nodes binned into 5 classes by
average monthly traffic. All six are used almost exclusively for node classification.

1.4 Social and interaction networks
These are the benchmarks of the unsupervised embedding literature (DeepWalk, LINE, node2vec).
They are typically feature-less and multi-label, which is why Micro-F1/Macro-F1 rather than
accuracy became the standard metric in that line of work.
BlogCatalog is a friendship network of bloggers labelled with overlapping interests and Flickr is
a contact network labelled with the groups each user joined; both are from Tang & Liu, Relational
Learning via Latent Social Dimensions, KDD 2009. YouTube is a user–user social network labelled
with group memberships, from the companion paper Tang & Liu, Scalable Learning of Collective
Behavior based on Sparse Social Dimensions, CIKM 2009, and is the scalability stress test of that
literature.
(Statistics for all three are as tabulated in the DeepWalk paper.)
None has node
attributes, so structure is the only signal. Reddit (Hamilton, Ying & Leskovec, NIPS 2017) links
two posts when the same user commented on both, labels each post with its community, and is the
standard inductive, large-scale node-classification benchmark.3
Warning — three names, six graphs. BlogCatalog, Flickr and PPI each denote two genuinely
different graphs in this literature, and numbers are frequently mixed up between them. The feature-
less multi-label versions above are the ones used by DeepWalk/node2vec; the attributed versions
(BlogCatalog with 5,196 nodes / 171,743 edges / 8,189 features / 6 classes; the GraphSAINT Flickr
with 89,250 nodes / 449,878 edges / 500 features / 7 classes) are the ones shipped by modern GNN
libraries. A paper’s numbers are only comparable to another’s if the same version is used.
1.5 Biological networks
PPI (protein–protein interaction, GraphSAGE version) is 24 separate graphs, one per human
tissue, whose node features are positional gene sets, motif gene sets and immunological signatures,
and whose 121 binary labels are gene ontology terms. The split is inductive across graphs — 20
train, 2 validate, 2 test — so the test graphs are never seen during training. This is the canonical
test of whether a method generalises to unseen graphs at all, which transductive methods such as
DeepWalk cannot do by construction. The node2vec PPI is a different graph: a single Homo
sapiens subgraph with 3,890 nodes, 76,584 edges and 50 labels.
1.6 The Open Graph Benchmark (OGB)
Hu et al. (Open Graph Benchmark: Datasets for Machine Learning on Graphs, NeurIPS 2020)
introduced OGB to fix the two standing problems with the classic benchmarks: they are tiny, and
their random splits are unrealistically easy. OGB fixes the split, fixes the metric, and provides
a shared evaluator, so numbers are comparable across papers.

1.7 Split protocols — and why they must be stated
A result is meaningless without its split, because the same model can differ by many accuracy
points across protocols on the same graph:
• Planetoid semi-supervised split: exactly 20 labelled nodes per class for training, 500
for validation, 1,000 for test. This is the split on which the familiar Cora ≈81.5% figure for
GCN is reported.
• Full-supervised / “Geom-GCN” split: 60/20/20% of nodes per class, averaged over 10
random splits. Standard for the heterophilous benchmarks and typically 5–10 points higher
than the 20-per-class numbers.
• Random splits with fixed label budget (Shchur et al.): the Planetoid budget but re-
randomised, reported as mean ± std over ≥100 splits. This was the paper’s central recom-
mendation, since the single fixed Planetoid split does not support the ranking of methods
usually claimed from it.
• OGB splits are fixed and non-random — by time, by sales rank, by species, by experimental
throughput, by protein target — and deliberately induce distribution shift between train and
test.
• Link prediction: edges, not nodes, are split. The message-passing graph must contain only
training edges, otherwise the held-out edge leaks into its own prediction. See Figure 3.

3. Baselines
3.1 Matrix factorisation and spectral methods
• Laplacian Eigenmaps — Belkin & Niyogi, Laplacian Eigenmaps and Spectral Techniques for
Embedding and Clustering, NIPS 2001; expanded as Laplacian Eigenmaps for Dimensionality
Reduction and Data Representation, Neural Computation 15(6):1373–1396, 2003 (the canonical
citation).
Embeds nodes by the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian L = D −A belonging
to the smallest non-zero eigenvalues, i.e. it minimises ∑︁
(u,v)∈E wuv∥f(u) −f(v)∥2 subject to
an orthogonality constraint ruling out the trivial solution. The ancestor of essentially every
proximity-preserving embedding; spectral clustering is the same computation.
• GraRep — Cao, Lu & Xu, GraRep: Learning Graph Representations with Global Structural
Information, CIKM 2015.
Observes that first-order proximity is not enough, and factorises
the k-step transition matrices P 1, . . . , P K (via SVD of their log-transformed positive entries)
separately, concatenating the K resulting embeddings. This captures k-hop structure explicitly,
at the cost of dense |V | × |V | powers.
• HOPE — Ou, Cui, Pei, Zhang & Zhu, Asymmetric Transitivity Preserving Graph Embedding,
KDD 2016.
Targets directed graphs, where proximity is asymmetric.

PageRank, Common Neighbors or Adamic–Adar — which it shows can be written in a sparse

3.2 Random-walk / skip-gram methods
• DeepWalk — Perozzi, Al-Rfou & Skiena, DeepWalk: Online Learning of Social Representations,
KDD 2014. The paper that started the field. It samples uniform random walks from each node,
treats each walk as a “sentence” and each node as a “word”, and runs word2vec/skip-gram with
hierarchical softmax over them. Nodes appearing in similar walk contexts receive similar vectors.
It requires no node attributes and is trivially parallel, but is transductive: unseen nodes have
no embedding.
• LINE — Tang, Qu, Wang, Zhang, Yan & Mei, LINE: Large-scale Information Network Embed-
ding, WWW 2015. Dispenses with walks and optimises two explicit objectives: first-order prox-
imity (observed edges, p1(u, v) = σ(f(u)⊤f(v))) and second-order proximity (shared neighbour-
hoods, a context embedding per node), each trained by negative sampling with edge-sampling
to handle weights. The two embeddings are concatenated. It scales to millions of nodes and is
the standard “no-walk” baseline.
• node2vec — Grover & Leskovec, node2vec: Scalable Feature Learning for Networks, KDD 2016.
Generalises DeepWalk by biasing the walk with return parameter p and in-out parameter q,

interpolating between breadth-first search (which captures structural equivalence) and depth-
first search (which captures homophily). Tuning (p, q) per dataset is what buys its consistent

• struc2vec — Ribeiro, Savarese & Figueiredo, struc2vec: Learning Node Representations from
Structural Identity, KDD 2017. Argues that nodes with similar roles should embed together even
if they are far apart in the graph — something no proximity-based method can do. It builds a
multilayer context graph whose layer-k edge weights compare the ordered degree sequences of
k-hop neighbourhoods, then runs skip-gram on walks over that auxiliary graph.
3.3 Autoencoder methods
• SDNE — Wang, Cui & Zhu, Structural Deep Network Embedding, KDD 2016. A deep au-
toencoder whose input is a node’s adjacency row. It combines a reconstruction loss on that
row (second-order proximity), re-weighted so that the many zeros do not dominate the few ones,
with a Laplacian-eigenmaps-style penalty tying connected nodes together (first-order proximity).
The first non-linear (deep) network embedding method.
• GAE / VGAE — Kipf & Welling, Variational Graph Auto-Encoders, NIPS 2016 Bayesian Deep
Learning Workshop. Uses a two-layer GCN as the encoder, Z = GCN(X, A), and a simple inner-
product decoder ˆA = σ(ZZ⊤), trained by reconstruction cross-entropy; the variational version
places a Gaussian prior on Z and adds a KL term. It is the default unsupervised baseline for
link prediction on Cora/CiteSeer/PubMed.

• SGC — Wu, Souza, Zhang, Fifty, Yu & Weinberger, Simplifying Graph Convolutional Net-

• APPNP — Gasteiger (formerly Klicpera), Bojchevski & Günnemann, Predict then Propagate:
Graph Neural Networks meet Personalized PageRank, ICLR 2019. Decouples prediction from
propagation: an MLP produces per-node predictions H, which are then diffused by a truncated
personalized-PageRank iteration Z(k+1) = (1 −α) ˆAZ(k) + αH. The teleport term α keeps the
node’s own signal alive, so a large propagation radius is possible without over-smoothing and
without extra parameters.
• GCNII — Chen, Wei, Huang, Ding & Li, Simple and Deep Graph Convolutional Networks,
ICML 2020. Makes genuinely deep GCNs (32–64 layers) work using two ingredients: an initial
residual connection to H(0) (the APPNP teleport idea) and identity mapping of the weight
matrix, (1 −βl)I + βlW (l). Proves the result still expresses a polynomial filter of arbitrary order
and so does not collapse to over-smoothing.
• SEAL — Zhang & Chen, Link Prediction Based on Graph Neural Networks, NeurIPS 2018.
Reframes link prediction as subgraph classification: for a candidate pair (u, v) it extracts the
h-hop enclosing subgraph, applies a Double-Radius Node Labeling that encodes each node’s
distance to both u and v, and classifies the labelled subgraph with a GNN. The γ-decaying
theory in the paper shows such a GNN can learn any of the classical heuristics below rather
than assuming one. The strongest general-purpose link-prediction baseline.
• NBFNet — Zhu, Zhang, Xhonneux & Tang, Neural Bellman-Ford Networks: A General Graph
Neural Network Framework for Link Prediction, NeurIPS 2021. Generalises the Bellman–Ford
path formulation by replacing (min, +) with learned Indicator, Message and Aggregate
operators, so the score for (u, v) is a learned sum over all paths from u to v. Inductive (it
depends on structure relative to u, not on node identity) and applies to homogeneous graphs
and knowledge graphs alike.

5. Summary
For homogeneous graphs the schema is degenerate — one node type, one edge type (Figure 1) — so
what distinguishes the benchmarks is scale, feature type, and above all label homophily (Eq. (1)).
The suite has moved in three waves: the small homophilous citation graphs with a fixed 20-per-class
split, the re-splittable co-authorship/co-purchase graphs and the heterophilous WebKB/Wikipedia
graphs introduced to expose the homophily assumption, and finally OGB, with realistic non-random
splits and a shared evaluator at a scale where sampling-based training is mandatory. On metrics,
the two points that matter most are that Micro-F1 equals accuracy in the single-label case while
Macro-F1 is what exposes neglected rare classes, and that on sparse graphs AUC is optimistic, so
ranking metrics against a fixed negative set (Hits@K, MRR) are what make link-prediction results
comparable.

1. Set-up: HINs, network schemas and meta-paths
Heterogeneous information network (HIN).
Following Sun et al. (PathSim, PVLDB
4(11):992–1003, 2011) and the survey of Shi, Li, Zhang, Sun & Yu (A Survey of Heterogeneous
Information Network Analysis, IEEE TKDE 29(1):17–37, 2017), a heterogeneous information
network is a graph
G = (V, E, ϕ, ψ),
ϕ : V →A,
ψ : E →R,
in which every node v ∈V carries exactly one node type ϕ(v) from the type set A, every edge e ∈E
carries exactly one relation type ψ(e) from R, and
|A| + |R| > 2
This inequality is the definition of heterogeneity: when |A| = |R| = 1 the network degenerates
to the homogeneous graph of Problem 1. Each relation is typed at both ends, inducing a map

vs. written-by), which is why the released benchmarks below count 6 or 8 “edge types” where a
reader might expect 3 or 4.
Network schema.
The network schema TG = (A, R) is the directed graph whose vertices are
the node types A and whose edges are the relation types R. It is a meta-template of which G is an
instantiation: every node and edge of G maps to some type in TG, so the schema states which kinds
of connection the dataset can express at all. This is the object Section 3 draws for each benchmark.
Note that the drawing carries real information here whereas it does not in the homogeneous case:
Cora’s schema is a single node type with one self-relation, while DBLP’s is a four-type star.

DBLP (bibliographic).
A computer-science bibliography extract with types author, paper, term,

ACM (bibliographic).
Papers from ACM venues with types paper, author, subject, term. Papers

Distinctively, ACM has a paper–paper citation relation in addition to the star

Types movie, director, actor and (in HGB) keyword. Movies are labelled by

The name denotes two unrelated graphs. HGB’s Freebase is a general knowledge graph:

4. Baselines
4.1 Homogeneous methods, reported as type-agnostic baselines
These are reported in essentially every heterogeneous paper, and the point of reporting them is
that they discard ϕ and ψ entirely — they run on the graph with all type information erased (or
on one meta-path-induced projection). They are the control condition: whatever a heterogeneous
model gains over them is the measured value of modelling types. HGB’s central and uncomfortable
finding is that this gap is much smaller than the literature had claimed, and that a properly tuned
GAT beats most specialised HGNNs.
• DeepWalk — Perozzi, Al-Rfou & Skiena, KDD 2014. Truncated uniform random walks
treated as sentences, fed to skip-gram with hierarchical softmax.
• LINE — Tang, Qu, Wang, Zhang, Yan & Mei, WWW 2015. Explicitly preserves first-order
(observed edge) and second-order (shared-neighbourhood) proximity, optimised with negative
sampling and edge-sampling for large graphs.
• node2vec — Grover & Leskovec, KDD 2016. DeepWalk with a biased second-order walk
whose p, q parameters interpolate between BFS-like (structural equivalence) and DFS-like
(homophily) exploration.
• GCN — Kipf & Welling, ICLR 2017. First-order spectral convolution: a layer is a symmet-
rically normalised neighbourhood average followed by a linear map and nonlinearity.

4.2 Meta-path and random-walk HIN embedding
• metapath2vec — Dong, Chawla & Swami, KDD 2017. Random walks are meta-path-guided:
at each step the walker must move to the node type prescribed by the meta-path scheme (e.g.
APVPA), so the generated context is type-balanced instead of being dominated by the most
frequent type. The walks feed a heterogeneous skip-gram. Note that the base model still
normalises negative sampling over all nodes regardless of type; it is the metapath2vec++
variant that normalises the softmax per node type.
• HIN2Vec — Fu, Lee & Lei, CIKM 2017. Rather than fixing meta-paths in advance, it learns

4.4 Self-supervised / contrastive heterogeneous methods
• DMGI — Park, Kim, Han & Yu, AAAI 2020. Extends Deep Graph Infomax to attributed
multiplex networks: one DGI mutual-information objective per relation type, with a consensus
regularisation that pulls the per-relation embeddings towards a shared representation.
• HDGI — Ren, Liu, Huang, Dai, Bo & Zhang, arXiv:1911.08538, 2019 (AAAI-20 DLGMA
workshop, under a different title; no archival venue). Applies mutual-information maximisa-
tion between local node embeddings and a global summary, using meta-path-based convolu-
tion plus semantic attention as the encoder.
• HeCo — Wang, Liu, Han & Shi, KDD 2021. Contrasts two views of the same node: a
network-schema view (attention over directly-connected neighbours of each other type) and a
meta-path view (meta-path-specific GCNs with semantic attention). Positives are not merely
the node’s counterpart view — HeCo selects multiple positives by counting how many meta-
path instances connect two nodes and taking the top ones.

5. Evaluation metrics
Two conventions govern the whole literature. First, a HIN benchmark normally labels exactly one
node type — the author in DBLP, the paper in ACM, the movie in IMDB — so “accuracy on
DBLP” always means accuracy on that one target type, the other types being context. Second, for
unsupervised methods the protocol is embed →freeze →fit a linear classifier: the embedding
is learned without labels, then held fixed while a logistic regression is trained on a fraction of target
nodes, the fraction varied over 20/40/60/80% and mean ± std reported over repeated splits. HAN,
MAGNN and HeCo all report in this form.

5.2 Link prediction
The essential difference from the homogeneous case is that in a HIN link prediction is stated per
relation type: not “is there an edge” but “is there an edge of type R” — predict Author–Paper,
or Paper–Venue, or User–Artist. Two consequences follow. (i) Negatives must be sampled type-
consistently: a negative for an Author–Paper edge must join an author to a paper, never an author
to a venue, since a type-violating negative is trivially rejected by any type-aware model and inflates
its apparent margin over type-agnostic baselines. HGB samples negatives from 2-hop neighbours
at a 1:1 ratio for exactly this reason. (ii) Scores are reported per relation and then averaged, so
a model can be strong on a dense relation and useless on a sparse one without that showing in a
single number.

5.3 Summary: dataset →target →task →metric
Closing remark.
The single most important methodological point is the one HGB was created
to make: because DBLP, ACM and IMDB circulate in mutually incompatible preprocessed ver-
sions (Table 6), and because IMDB even changes from single-label to multi-label between versions,
numbers copied across papers are frequently not comparable. Any credible evaluation must name
the dataset version, the split, and whether the protocol is end-to-end supervised or embed-freeze-
classify.
