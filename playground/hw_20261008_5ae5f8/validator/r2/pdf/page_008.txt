Critique.
The results are tied to one machine, in both directions. The Alpha’s four page
sizes are what the incremental design exploits, and Table 2 shows what survives with only the
largest: C4 falls from 1.36 to 0.98, SP from 1.19 to 0.98, mcf from 1.68 to 1.22. Section 2.2
names the i386 family as offering exactly two sizes and never returns to what that implies for
the era’s dominant platform. Conversely the overheads are inflated by Alpha PTE replication,
charged with 7.2 of the adversary’s 8.9 percentage points.
The abstract’s “these benefits are sustained even under stressful workload scenarios” is
likewise broader than the evidence: the long-term case replays one web access log and runs
one benchmark four times, and the concurrent experiment substitutes a dummy allocator and
disables promotion altogether, measuring contiguity restored rather than speedup sustained.
Demoting on write is a frank retreat and the authors say so, that it “may deny the benefits
of superpages to processes that do not write to all of the base pages”. The weaker move is the
alternative they discard: hashing base pages to find which were really modified was rejected on
preliminary microbenchmarks costing up to 15%, and 60% on a saturated sequential read, yet
the same paragraph lists two optimisations, hashing only partially dirty superpages and moving
the work into the idle loop, that attack exactly that cost and are left to future work. An option
dismissed on its least optimised form has not been evaluated.
Ex. 3.10
(20 pts) — three papers found by search
The three papers below come from Google, Meta and Microsoft, and each answers a question
the 1968–2002 literature never faced: DRAM is now the largest single line in server cost, so
which pages leave it, and on what evidence? The first two report fleet-wide deployments; the
third is a design study on emulated hardware. They also disagree with one another.
1. Software-defined far memory at Google
Andres Lagar-Cavilla, Junwhan Ahn, Suleiman Souhlal, Neha Agarwal, Radoslaw
Burny, Shakeel Butt, Jichuan Chang, Ashwin Chaugule, Nan Deng, Junaid Shahid,
Greg Thelen, Kamil Adam Yurtsever, Yu Zhao and Parthasarathy Ranganathan,
“Software-Defined Far Memory in Warehouse-Scale Computers”, Proc. 24th Int. Conf. on Archi-
tectural Support for Programming Languages and Operating Systems (ASPLOS ’19), Providence,
RI, April 2019, pp. 317–330. Chosen because it is the first end-to-end report of a cold-page
policy running on a whole fleet rather than a testbed.
Summary.
A cheap, slow memory tier below DRAM only pays if cold pages can be found
without slowing anything down, and a fleet measurement shows why fixed hardware will not do
it: at a cold-age threshold of T = 120 s, 32% of memory is cold on average but the per-machine
figure ranges from 1% to 52% within a single cluster, so any provisioned far-memory capacity
would be stranded on some machines and exhausted on others. The authors therefore build
the tier in software, from zswap, which keeps compressed pages in DRAM. Upstream zswap
fires only on direct reclaim, making its cost unbounded and bursty, so the contribution is a
control plane that compresses proactively instead. A kernel daemon kstaled sweeps page-table
accessed bits every 120 s and keeps an 8-bit age per page inside struct page; a second daemon
kreclaimd compresses pages older than a per-job threshold. The threshold is chosen against
a service-level objective expressed in an application-agnostic indicator: the promotion rate,
or swap-ins per minute, held below 0.2% of the job’s working set per minute. A node agent
